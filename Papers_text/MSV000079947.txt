=== TITLE ===
Proteome profiling of breast cancer biopsies reveals a wound healing signature of cancer-associated fibroblasts.

=== ABSTRACT ===
Breast cancer is still the most common type of cancer in women; an important role in carcinogenesis is actually attributed to cancer-associated fibroblasts. In this study, we investigated whether it is possible to assess the functional state of cancer-associated fibroblasts through tumor tissue proteome profiling. Tissue proteomics was performed on tumor-central, tumor-near, and tumor-distant biopsy sections from breast adenocarcinoma patients, which allowed us to identify 2074 proteins. Data were interpreted referring to reference proteome profiles generated from primary human mammary fibroblasts comprising 4095 proteins. These cells were analyzed in quiescent cell state as well as after in vitro treatment with TGFβ or IL-1β, stimulating wound healing or inflammatory processes, respectively. Representative for cancer cells, we investigated the mammary carcinoma cell line ZR-75-1, identifying 5212 proteins. All mass analysis data have been made fully accessible via ProteomeXchange, DOI PXD001311 and PXD001323-8. Comparison of tissue proteomics data with all of those reference profiles revealed predominance of cancer cell-derived proteins within the tumor and fibroblast-derived proteins in the tumor-distant tissue sections. Remarkably, proteins characteristic for acute inflammation were hardly identified in the tissue samples. In contrast, several proteins found by us to be induced by TGFβ in mammary fibroblasts, including fibulin-5, SLC2A1, and MUC18, were positively identified in all tissue samples, with relatively higher abundance in tumor neighboring tissue sections. These findings indicate a predominance of cancer-associated fibroblasts with wound healing activities localized around tumors.

=== INTRODUCTION ===


=== METHODS ===
The study was approved by the Ethics Commission of the Medical University of Vienna (application number 1074/2011), and after informed consent was given, samples were taken from breast cancer patients by ultrasound guided needle biopsies. The patients (three postmenopausal women, aged 64 to 75) were diagnosed with invasive breast carcinoma with a histological grade of 2; the mammographies were all classified as highly suggestive of malignancy (BI-RADS 5). All patients were estrogen-receptor- and progesterone-receptor-positive as well as human epidermal growth-factor-2-negative. Each obtained tissue was divided in tumor-central, tumor-near, and tumor-distant sections; tumor-near sections were cut in a radius within <1 cm from the tumor and tumor-distant sections at a distance between 1 and 3 cm from the tumor. Biopsy excision was performed using ultrasound scanning for appropriate localization of the tumor. An example of an ultrasound guided needle biopsy is represented in Figure 1 for one patient. The biopsies were 1.5 mm in diameter and 15 mm in length, corresponding to 19 to 44 mg of isolated tissue material. The samples were immediately processed after puncture by the surgeon (G.P.) and differentiated into tumor-central, tumor-near, and tumor-distant sections. The borders from tumor-central to tumor-near were obvious from macroscopic tissue morphology. The distinction of tumor-near and tumor-far was based on the ultrasound guided needle positioning. Biopsies were directly put into sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and processed immediately as described later.

Cell Culture
Human breast cancer cells ZR-75-1 were cultured as described previously. (14) HMFs were obtained from ScienCell Research Labortories (Carlsbad, CA) and cultured in 25 cm2 flasks using 4 mL of Fibroblast Basal Medium (Lonza, Switzerland) containing 10% FCS. Stimulation was carried out for 7 days with TGFβ at a concentration of 2 ng/mL or for 24 h with IL-1β at a concentration of 10 ng/mL. At the end of the stimulation period, the medium was changed to serum-free RPMI1640 for 4 h. For HMFs, experiments were carried out in biological duplicates.
Sample Preparation
Tissue samples were homogenized with a glass homogenizer and transferred to plastic tubes. To obtain the cytoplasmic fraction, tissue samples as well as cultivated cells were lysed in isotonic lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES/NaOH, pH 7.4, 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitors (pepstatin, leupeptin, and aprotinin, each at 1 μg/mL; 1 mM PMSF) and mechanical shear stress as previously described. (15) By centrifugation at 2300g and 4 °C for 5 min, the cytoplasmic proteins were separated from the nuclei and precipitated overnight with ice-cold ethanol at −20 °C. After precipitation, proteins were dissolved in sample buffer (7.5 M urea, 1.5 M thiourea, 4% CHAPS, 0.05% SDS, 100 mM DTT); the determination of protein concentration was carried out using a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad-Laboratories, Germany). 20 μg of proteins was separated by SDS-PAGE and thereafter silver-stained. Each protein band was cut into eight slices (tissue samples) or four slices (cultured cells), and proteins were subsequently digested enzymatically using trypsin (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) upon reduction with dithiothreitol and alkylation with iodoacetamide as previously described. (16, 17) The resulting peptides were eluted and stored at −20 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.
LC–MS/MS Analysis
Samples were resuspended in 4 μL of 30% formic acid (FA) containing four synthetic standard peptides as internal standards and 40 μL of mobile phase A (98% H2O, 2% ACN, 0.2% FA), and 10 μL thereof was injected into the LC system (Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria) equipped with a 2 cm × 75 μm C18 Pepmap100 precolumn and a 15 cm × 75 μm Pepmap100 column in the case of tissue samples or a 50 cm × 75 μm Pepmap100 column (all Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria) in the case of cultured cells. Samples were loaded on the precolumn at a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 10 min using 100% mobile phase A. Peptides were then eluted with a flow rate of 300 nL/min from the precolumn to the analytical column and separated with a gradient of 1–40% mobile phase B (20% H2O, 80% ACN, 0.2% FA) over 45 min for tissue samples, 185 min for HMFs, and 235 min for ZR-75-1. Mass spectrometric analysis of tissue samples and ZR-75-1 cells was achieved on a QExactive Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria) equipped with a nanospray ion source. A precursor ion scan in the range from m/z 400–1400 (resolution 70 000 at m/z = 200) was followed by MS/MS scans of the six most abundant ions upon fragmentation in the HCD cell at 30% normalized collision energy (isolation width of Δm/z = 2, resolution 17 500 at m/z = 200). In the case of HMFs, an LTQ Orbitrap Velos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria) also equipped with a nanospray ion source was used for MS analysis. The orbitrap was used for precursor ion scans in the range from m/z 400–1400 (resolution 60 000 at m/z = 400). MS/MS analysis was performed using the ion trap (isolation width of Δm/z = 3). Therefore, the six most abundant ions were fragmented in the CID cell at 35% normalized collision energy. All samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS as duplicates. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium (18) via the proteomics identification database (PRIDE) repository with the data set identifiers PXD001311 and PXD001324-8 (DOI 10.6019/PXD001311 and 10.6019/PXD001324–8; Supplemetary Table S1 in the Supporting Information).
LC–MS/MS Data Processing
Protein identification was carried out using ProteomeDiscoverer 1.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austria) running Mascot 2.4 (Matrix Science, U.K.) by searching against the SwissProt Database (version December 2012 with 20 209 protein entries) supplemented with common contaminant proteins. Search criteria included 5 ppm mass tolerance for MS spectra, 20 mmu mass tolerance for MS/MS spectra when using the Q Exactive and 0.6 Da when using the Velos orbitrap, a maximum of two allowed missed cleavages, fixed carbamidomethyl–cysteine modifications, variable methionine oxidation and N-terminal protein acetylation, Mascot significance threshold of 0.05, and a false discovery rate of <0.01. LFQ analysis of cytoplasmic fractions was carried out using MaxQuant 1.3.0.5, allowing a maximum of 5 ppm initial mass deviation for precursor ion spectra and 20 ppm for MS/MS spectra when using the Q Exactive and 0.5 Da when using the Velos orbitrap. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues was set as a fixed modification, oxidation of methionine residues and N-terminal protein acetylation were set as variable modifications. Data were searched against the Uniprot database (version July 2012 with 20 209 protein entries). FDR for peptide and protein identification was set to 0.01, and results obtained from MaxQuant were further analyzed using Perseus statistical package (version 1.3.0.4; part of the MaxQuant distribution). (19, 20) Protein identifications were filtered for common contaminants. Proteins were only considered for quantification if they were found in a minimum of two-thirds of all samples belonging to a sample group (control, IL-1β-stimulated, TGFβ-stimulated for cell-culture samples; tumor, tumor-near, and tumor-distant for patient samples). Significantly changed protein abundance was determined by two-sided t test with p < 0.01 (permutation-based FDR correction). Euclidean distance and average linkage clustering was used for hierarchical clustering of z-scored LFQ values. Enrichment analysis was based on gene ontology terms for molecular functions and biological processes.
