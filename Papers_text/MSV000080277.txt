=== TITLE ===
Proteomic analysis of cerebrospinal fluid extracellular vesicles: A comprehensive dataset

=== ABSTRACT ===
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are present in human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), yet little is known about their protein composition. The aim of this study is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the proteome of CSF EVs by electron microscopy and high resolution tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) in conjunction with bioinformatics. We report an extensive catalog of 1315 proteins identified in EVs isolated from two different CSF pools by ultracentrifugation, including 230 novel EV proteins. Out of 1315 proteins, 760 were identified in both CSF pools and about 30% of those were also quantitatively enriched in the EV fraction versus the soluble CSF fraction. The proteome of CSF EVs was enriched in exosomal markers such as alix and syntenin-1, heat shock proteins and tetraspanins and contained a high proportion of brain-derived proteins (n = 373). Interestingly, several known biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases such as the amyloid precursor protein, the prion protein and DJ-1 were identified in the EV fractions. Our dataset represents the first comprehensive inventory of the EV proteome in CSF, underscoring the biomarker potential of this organelle. Further comparative studies on CSF EVs isolated from patients diagnosed with neurological disorders are warranted. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier PXD000608.

=== METHODS ===
2.1. CSF samples and microvesicle isolation
Two independent pools (CSF1 and CSF2) of 6 ml of CSF were used for the proteomic analysis. CSF aliquots (0.5 mL) were derived of residual samples used in routine diagnostics and stored at − 80 °C. EV isolation was performed as previously reported with minor changes [6]. Briefly, about 6 mL of CSF was centrifuged at 500 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove any intact cells, the supernatant was carefully collected and spun at 2000 ×g spin for 15 min and finally at 20,000 ×g for 45 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 2 h in an ultracentrifuge using a 40Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter, Woerden, The Netherlands). The soluble CSF (supernatant, SN) was removed and the pellets containing EVs were resuspended in phosphate buffer saline (PBS), carefully washed and centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 2 h to collect final EV pellets. All the centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C. Sucrose cushion isolation of CSF EVs was performed as previously reported [6]. Pellets were dissolved in sample buffer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA cat No: NP0008) for proteomics and western blot experiments. SN fraction (~ 5 mL) was concentrated to 100 μL using 3 kDa filters (Millipore, Billerica, CA, USA) and then dissolved in sample buffer 2 ×.
2.2. Electron microscopy and immunolabeling
EVs isolated from 4 mL of CSF were resuspended in 30 μL of a solution composed of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. After 10 minute permeabilization in 0.1% saponin, the EVs were spotted on carbon/formvar-coated EM grids and air-dried. Samples were fixed to the EM grid in 1% PFA, washed and labeled for CD63 (BD Bioscience, NJ, USA) using 10 nm Protein A-gold (CMC Utrecht, The Netherlands). The grids were contrasted by 2% uranyl acetate and analyzed in JEOL1010 TEM. Size measurements were performed with ImageJ (NIH).
2.3. Western blotting
The following antibodies were used for Western blotting: alix (monoclonal, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), flotillin 1 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), and CD63 (monoclonal, 1:500 BD Biosciences). EV isolated from 4 ml of CSF and corresponding SN fractions (30 μg) was denatured by boiling for 5 min and loaded onto 4–12%. NuPAGE Bis-Tris gel, (1 mm × 15 wells). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes for 2 h. Membranes were blocked for 1 h RT with blocking buffer (5% non-fat milk in PBS — Tween20 0.1%) then incubated overnight with the antibodies, diluted in blocking buffer. After washing with PBS-T three times, the membranes were incubated for 1 h RT with secondary antibody, which was either anti-mouse or anti-rabbit (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. Blots were washed three times with PBS-T and then developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (GE healthcare).
2.4. SDS-PAGE and in-gel digestion
For proteomic analysis EV and SN (30 μg) fractions were loaded on gradient gels from Invitrogen (NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel, 1 mm × 10 wells). The gels were then stained with Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Before MS analysis, separated proteins were in-gel digested as previously described [15]. Briefly, gels were washed and dehydrated once in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) and twice in 50 mM ABC/50% acetonitrile (ACN). Cysteine bonds were reduced by incubation with 10 mM DTT/50 mM ABC at 56 °C for 1 h and alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide/50 mM ABC at room temperature (RT) in the dark for 45 min. After washing sequentially with ABC and ABC/50% ACN, the whole gel was sliced in 9 bands for each lane. Gel parts were sliced up into approximately 1-mm cubes and collected in tubes, washed in ABC/ACN and dried in a vacuum centrifuge. Gel cubes were incubated overnight at 23 °C with 6.25 ng/mL trypsin and covered with ABC to allow digestion. Peptides were extracted once in 1% formic acid and twice in 5% formic acid/50% ACN. The volume of the peptide extract was reduced to 60 μL in a vacuum centrifuge and filtered using a 0.45 μm filter to remove gel particles and contaminants prior to LC–MS analysis.
2.5. NanoLC–MS/MS analysis and database searching
Extracted peptides were separated on a 75 μm × 42 cm custom packed Reprosil C18 aqua column (1.9 μm, 120 Å) in a 90 min. gradient (2–32% Acetonitrile + 0.5% Acetic acid at 300 nL/min) using a U3000 RSLC high pressure nanoLC (Dionex). Eluting peptides were measured on-line by a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) operating in data-dependent acquisition mode. Peptides were ionized using a stainless-steel emitter at a potential of + 2 kV (Thermo Fischer Scientific). Intact peptide ions were detected at a resolution of 35,000 (at m/z 200) and fragment ions at a resolution of 17,500 (at m/z 200); the MS mass range was 350–1500 Da. Automatic gain control target settings for MS were 3E6 charges and for MS/MS 2E5 charges. Peptides were selected for higher-energy C-trap dissociation fragmentation at an underfill ratio of 1% and a quadrupole isolation window of 1.5 Da, peptides were fragmented at a normalized collision energy of 30. Raw files from MS analysis were processed using the MaxQuant computational proteomics platform version 1.3.0.5 [16]. MS/MS spectra were searched against the Uniprot human database (release 2013_1 January 9th, 2013, excluding fragment, 43,688 sequences) with initial precursor and fragment mass tolerance set to 7 and 20 p.p.m., respectively. Peptides with minimum of seven amino-acid length were considered with both the peptide and protein false discovery rate (FDR) set to 1%. Enzyme specificity was set to trypsin and up to two missed cleavage sites were allowed. Cysteine carbamidomethylation (Cys, + 57.021464 Da) was searched as a fixed modification, whereas N-acetylation of proteins (N-terminal, + 42.010565 Da), oxidized methionine (Met, + 15.994915 Da), and serine, threonine, and tyrosine phosphorylations (Ser/Thr/Tyr, + 79.966331 Da) were searched as variable modifications.
2.6. Statistical enrichment and bioinformatics analyses
Protein group output file from MaxQuant was processed with an in-house R-script and fold changes between EV and SN fractions of the two CSF pools were calculated. To evaluate statistical enrichment of spectral counts for proteins in EV and SN fractions we used a beta binomial paired test [17]. Functional annotations of identified proteins were found using different tools. General ontologies and tissue expression data were retrieved using DAVID knowledgebase [18] while the GOrilla webserver was used to compare different lists and evaluate the ranked list of EV proteins [19]. Hierarchical clustering of gene ontology term was performed with the Cluster software [20].
Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA), Uniprot (http://www.uniprot.org/) and Nextprot (http://www.nextprot.org/) databases were used to retrieve disease association of EV proteins. Associations were obtained for the following neurological disease: Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD). To obtain functional networks of the identified proteins we used the STRING database [21]. Functional and physical interactions were exported to a spreadsheet file, while visualization and network analyses were performed in Cytoscape (v 2.8.3). The final network was clustered using the MCL algorithm [22] embedded in clusterMaker Cytoscape plugin v 1.1 [23]. Subnetworks were searched for functional annotations using BiNGO plug-in [24].
